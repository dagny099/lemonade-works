# answer key

## reconstruction choices

The most defensible common denominator is an estimated **780 cups for the six-week window**:

- wk 1: 131
- wk 2: 127 after treating the market entry of 67 as a transposition of 76
- wk 5: 132
- wk 6: 130
- logged average: (131 + 127 + 132 + 130) / 4 = 130 cups/wk
- estimated six weeks: 130 × 6 = 780 cups

Using the raw wk 2 row without correcting it gives 118 for that week and a lower six-week estimate. That is possible, but the written subtotal, the other market counts, and the stated operating pattern make 76 the stronger reconstruction.

## cup cost — treatment 1: careful incremental manufacturing cost

This treatment removes the two likely duplicate receipt lines, treats the cooler as equipment rather than an ingredient consumed by the order, and leaves $5 of the sugar purchase in inventory based on the undated “maybe 5 lb left” note.

Deduplicated making-related outlays other than the cooler:

- 137 + 128 + 20 + 137 + 50 + 50 + 149 + 149 + 149 + 137 + 149 = 1,255
- less estimated unused sugar: 5
- cost assigned to cups made: 1,250

Arithmetic:

- 1,250 / 780 = **$1.6026 per cup**, rounded to **$1.60**

This is an incremental decision treatment. It does not charge the order for the already-purchased cooler and does not charge it for sugar still on hand.

## cup cost — treatment 2: naive cash-outlay cost

This treatment still removes the two obvious duplicate entries, but it folds the entire cooler purchase into the six-week cost and charges the entire 50 lb sugar purchase to the period in which it was bought.

- making-related cash outlays including cooler: 1,435
- 1,435 / 780 = **$1.8397 per cup**, rounded to **$1.84**

Comparison:

- (1.8397 - 1.6026) / 1.6026 = **14.8%**
- The two results are therefore within the requested roughly-15% band.

A person who also summed the suspected duplicate entries would get a much higher number. That is not one of the two defensible treatments; it is a data-cleaning failure.

## 200-cup inquiry

The inquiry offers **$340 total for 200 covered cups**.

### Under the careful treatment

- 200 × 1.6026 = 320.51
- 340 - 320.51 = **$19.49 remaining**

On the recorded manufacturing costs alone, the order is worth taking, though only narrowly.

### Under the naive treatment

- 200 × 1.8397 = 367.95
- 340 - 367.95 = **-$27.95**

Under this treatment, the order is not worth taking.

The rate therefore falls between the two unit-cost estimates and produces opposite decisions.

## planted inconsistencies and record defects

1. **Weeks 3 and 4 are absent from the sales log.**  
   Chosen because a skipped backfill is one of the most ordinary ways a small operator loses continuity. It forces estimation rather than allowing a clean six-week total.

2. **The wk 2 market count is 67, while the written subtotal only works if it is 76.**  
   Chosen as a common digit transposition. The row sum is 118, but the owner wrote 127. It creates a judgment call without looking like a designed riddle.

3. **The 6/22 Eastside Produce expense appears twice.**  
   The second copy has no date and is marked “already entered??” Chosen to model uncertainty during manual receipt transcription.

4. **The 7/16 PackRight order appears twice.**  
   The duplicate has neither date nor vendor and says “from email?” Chosen because an email confirmation and a receipt are easily mistaken for separate purchases.

5. **Several expense rows have no date.**  
   The HEB consumables, sugar purchase, one produce duplicate, one cups duplicate, and permit lack usable dates. This prevents a perfectly reliable period assignment.

6. **Several expense rows have no vendor.**  
   The extra lemons, suspected cups duplicate, and chalk markers have blank vendor fields. This makes matching receipts to supplier messages less certain.

7. **The expense date 6/31 cannot exist.**  
   It was chosen as the ordinary “typed the month-end date from memory” error. The intended date is most likely 6/30, but that is not explicitly recoverable.

8. **The equipment note calls 6/24 “thurs,” but June 24, 2026 was a Wednesday.**  
   Chosen as a casual weekday/date mismatch in a note written after the fact.

9. **The packet says the booth fee is $20, while the scratch sheet repeatedly records $22.**  
   The notes ask whether the extra $2 is a card charge. The records do not establish whether the rule changed, a surcharge was added, or the owner misremembered.

10. **The packet and coordinator email disagree on setup timing.**  
    The packet permits setup until 7:45 and says cars out by 7:50. The email says the booth must be ready by 7:30 and cars out by 7:40. This could be an update, a one-day instruction, or inconsistent guidance.

11. **The supplier price increase says the $149 total starts in July, while the scratch sheet records $149 on the impossible date 6/31.**  
    If the intended date was 6/30, the expense predates the stated effective date by a day. If the owner meant 7/1, the month is wrong.

12. **The inventory count says three cup sleeves plus loose cups are “maybe 120 total,” while the notes question whether a sleeve contains 40 or 50.**  
    Three full sleeves alone would be either 120 or 150, before any loose cups. The count is therefore not arithmetically stable.

13. **The inbox confirmation says the $137 PackRight order included two straw boxes, while the expense description says only “400 cups + lids.”**  
    Chosen because receipt descriptions often omit secondary items. It makes category allocation slightly uncertain without changing the cash total.

14. **The inventory sheet has 220 lids but only an ambiguous roughly-120 cup count.**  
    This could be leftover lids from prior sleeves, a counting error, or a genuine mismatch. It was included because paired consumables rarely reconcile perfectly.

15. **The sugar records do not establish a clean ending inventory date.**  
    The inventory count says about 17 lb on 7/10, while an undated note says maybe 5 lb remained. The latter can support a period-end estimate, but its timing cannot be proved.

16. **The market-day sales note says “maybe 2 comps?” but the cup count does not say whether comps were included.**  
    Chosen because operators often mix units served and units paid for. It affects revenue reconstruction, though the requested sales file intentionally contains no revenue.

- The exact date of every undated expense and whether each falls inside the six-week window.
- Absolute proof that the two suspicious entries are duplicates rather than repeated same-amount purchases.
